# Economy and governance progression

Where the Create ladder maps what you can build, this one maps how you trade, settle, and govern. Under
regional scarcity no one is self-sufficient, so trade and specialization are the spine of the pack, and the
long climb runs from lone barter up to a governed civilization that taxes, charters, and mints. The early
rungs are live today; the governing capstone is where the pack is headed. This page is the player's map of
that climb.

## How the climb feels

The early rungs are quiet and personal: scarcity makes self-reliance expensive, so you trade hand to hand
for what your region lacks. The middle is where money and a real marketplace replace barter, and where you
stop wandering and found a settlement. The far end — planned, not yet in game — is collective: settlements
become governed realms, realms federate into kingdoms and empires, and the largest powers tax, license, and
coin. Nothing is gated by a timer; you are gated by what you have built, what your ground holds, and who you
have allied with. More than the Create climb, this is the pack's multiplayer heart — most of its later rungs
only mean something alongside other people.

## The economic ladder

1. **Barter under scarcity** *(live)*. No region holds everything, so the first economy is the oldest one:
   you trade hand to hand for the metals and goods your ground lacks. Scarcity is the foundation the whole
   ladder stands on — it is what makes trade necessary rather than optional.
2. **Coin and banking** *(live)*. Numismatics turns metal into money: coinage pressed on a Create-powered
   mint, bank accounts, and spending cards. Money replaces the friction of barter and lets value move
   without a face-to-face swap. The mint runs on Create — the economy's first tie back into the tech spine.
3. **The marketplace** *(live)*. Create: Trading Floor adds stalls and standing orders — a place to post
   goods, fill a buyer's request, and let trade run while you are away. Coins plus a market make an economy,
   not just an exchange.
4. **Settlement** *(live)*. MineColonies is the settler's route: found a colony and let citizens work it. It
   is the cheaper, faster road to basic goods than building every machine yourself, which is exactly why a
   colony player and a Create player have reason to trade. A settlement climbs its own ladder — hamlet to
   village to town to city — as its citizens and buildings grow.
5. **Claimed ground** *(live)*. Open Parties and Claims marks what is yours and protects it; aeroclaims does
   the same for an airship. Claims are the lines the governing layer above is later drawn over.
6. **Governed realms** *(planned)*. Bind a settlement into a realm and give it law. A realm's laws are not
   invisible walls — make a place no-PvP and the colony's own guards turn on whoever draws first; ban a good
   and carrying it marks you. Where no guard stands, a law is only as strong as the players willing to
   enforce it, and a lawless frontier stays a real place. Realms appoint officers and citizens, so who may
   legislate and who must obey is itself part of the game.
7. **Federation and war** *(planned)*. Realms combine into counties, kingdoms, and empires. A higher tier's
   law sets the floor its members cannot override, and a great power may carve subordinate regions beneath
   it. Membership is voluntary — you may leave at any time — but leaving a realm that forbade it is an act of
   war, and war means a rival's people are fair game on your soil and yours on theirs.
8. **Factions and armies** *(planned)*. Standing powers you can pledge to, faction colors and kit, and
   soldiers raised from a colony's own guards — marched, rallied, and sent to siege a rival. This is the
   military arm of the governing era, the teeth behind a realm's borders and a federation's quarrels.
9. **Treasury, charters, and minting** *(planned, furthest out)*. The highest tiers turn political power into
   economic power: they tax the trade that crosses them, pay their citizens a stipend, and license special
   enterprises — a mine, a farm, an airship outpost — by charter, taking tribute in return. The last and
   least-settled rung is the mint: a great realm issuing its own currency, backed by its reserves, to
   circulate through the marketplace below. Expect this piece last.

## Logistics and aeronautics — moving the goods

An economy is only as wide as its reach. Airships and trains are the matter-moving arm of the economy: they
carry goods between regions and markets that scarcity has kept apart. A claimed airship can be more than
freight — it can be a mobile outpost, and a realm's flagship carries its own law onto foreign ground like an
embassy. The balance the pack holds to: freight and flight bridge distance at a real cost in fuel and
building, so far trade becomes possible without becoming free. If anyone could move anything anywhere for
nothing, regional scarcity — the thing that makes trade worth doing — would collapse. See
[create-progression](create-progression.md) for how aircraft are built and flown.

## Playing alone

A lone player can coin, settle, and claim — the live rungs stand on their own. But governance, federation,
and war are about how a group organizes itself, and the pack is balanced for a small cooperative group. The
economy is where that group becomes a society; it is the part of the pack that rewards having other people
around, not the part that works in spite of them.

## What is live, and what is planned

Rungs 1 through 5 are in the pack today, built on Numismatics, Create: Trading Floor, MineColonies, Open
Parties and Claims, and Create Aeronautics. Rungs 6 through 9 — realms, federation, factions and armies, and
the treasury-and-mint layer — are the pack's planned direction, added as dedicated content over the coming
versions rather than shipped now. They are mapped here so the destination is clear. Expect them in stages,
governing before coining: the law-and-realm layer first, the minting layer last.
